You’ve got the dough, the royal icing, and the Cricut Maker. But your stencil letters keep tearing or bleeding. The culprit? Your font choice.

Cookie fonts and script fonts look similar on screen, but they cut and weed very differently for Cricut stencil projects. This guide compares them head-to-head so you pick the right one every time.

## Key Takeaways

- Cookie fonts have thicker, connected strokes that weed faster and resist tearing.
- Script fonts have thinner, flowing lines that require more precision and often break.
- For Cricut stencils, cookie fonts are the safer choice for beginners and large batches.
- Script fonts work best for single, detailed cookies with steady hands.
- Stencil material (like mylar or acetate) changes which font type performs better.

## Cookie Font vs Script Font: What’s the Difference?

Cookie fonts are designed specifically for stencil-based cookie decorating. They have bold, uniform stroke widths and open counters (the holes in letters like “a” or “e”). This makes them cut cleanly on a Cricut and hold up during weeding.

Script fonts mimic handwriting. They have variable stroke thickness, connecting swashes, and thin hairlines. While beautiful, these thin parts are fragile when cut as a stencil.

### The Core Difference: Stroke Consistency

Cookie fonts keep strokes between 2mm and 4mm wide. That’s thick enough to support the stencil material without crumbling.

Script fonts often dip below 1mm in thin sections. Those hairlines tear easily when you peel away the stencil.

**Example:** The letter “n” in a cookie font has a solid 3mm curve. In a script font, the same “n” tapers to a 0.5mm point at the top.

## How Font Type Affects Cricut Stencil Weeding Time

Weeding is the most tedious part of stencil making. Font choice directly impacts how long you spend pulling out tiny pieces.

### Cookie Font Weeding: Fast and Frustration-Free

Because cookie fonts are thick and open, you can peel away the negative space in one or two big pieces. Islands (like the center of “o”) are large enough to grab with tweezers.

**Steps for weeding a cookie font stencil:**
1. Cut the stencil on a standard grip mat with mylar or acetate.
2. Lift one edge of the outer stencil frame.
3. Peel the entire negative space in one motion. The thick bridges hold together.
4. Use tweezers to remove islands. Each one is at least 3mm wide.

### Script Font Weeding: Slow and Delicate

Script fonts create dozens of tiny islands and narrow strips. You’ll spend 5–10 minutes per stencil picking out pieces that break apart.

**Common script font weeding issues:**
- Hairlines snap when you try to lift them.
- Swashes create long, thin curves that curl up and stick to themselves.
- Islands in “e” and “a” are so small they vanish when you touch them.

| Feature | Cookie Font | Script Font |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Stroke width | 2–4mm, consistent | 0.5–5mm, varies |
| Weeding time per stencil | 1–3 minutes | 5–15 minutes |
| Tear risk | Low | High |
| Best for | Large batches, beginners | Single detailed cookies |
| Stencil material | Mylar, acetate, vinyl | Mylar only (thin material) |
| Cut settings | Standard pressure, blade depth 2–3 | Less pressure, blade depth 1–2 |

## Cut Complexity: Which Font Cuts Cleaner on a Cricut?

Your Cricut cuts the same path whether the font is cookie or script. But the *shapes* within that path determine if cuts are clean or rough.

### Cookie Font Cut Paths: Simple and Smooth

Cookie fonts use geometric curves with few anchor points. The Cricut blade follows a predictable line. You get crisp edges without jagged bits.

**Tip:** Set cut pressure to “standard” and multi-cut to 2 for thicker stencil materials like acetate.

### Script Font Cut Paths: Complex and Risky

Script fonts have many anchor points to create flowing curves. More anchor points mean more chances for the blade to lift or skip. Thin sections often cut incompletely.

**Fix:** Use a fine-point blade and reduce speed to “slow” in the custom setting. This helps the blade follow tight curves without tearing.

## Which Font Stencil Holds Up During Icing Application?

The real test comes when you flood icing over the stencil. A weak stencil buckles, leaks, or lifts off the cookie.

### Cookie Font Stencil Stability

Thick bridges (the walls between open spaces) keep the stencil flat. Even with thin royal icing, the stencil won’t warp.

**Best practice:** Use a cookie font with at least 3mm stroke width for flooding. Let the icing set for 15 seconds before lifting the stencil.

### Script Font Stencil Stability

Thin walls in script fonts can’t hold their shape. Icing seeps under the stencil, creating blurry edges. The stencil may peel up when you lift it.

![Crafter weeding a stencil with tweezers on a bright lightbox surface](https://i.ibb.co/Ps3PRK52/site1-cookie-font-vs-script-font-cricut-stencils-body.jpg)

**Workaround:** Use a thicker icing consistency (15–20 second flood) and lift the stencil carefully with a toothpick.

## Design Tips for Choosing the Right Font

Your project scale and skill level should guide your choice.

### When to Use a Cookie Font

- You’re making 20+ cookies for an event.
- You’re new to stenciling with a Cricut.
- You’re using a stencil material thicker than 0.1mm.
- You want crisp, clean edges every time.

**Recommended cookie fonts:** Honey (available on [Creative Fabrica](https://www.creativefabrica.com/product/honey-131/ref/8793785/?campaign=fonts2&utm_source=site1&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=2026-07-29)), Cookie Monster, Stencil Sans.

### When to Use a Script Font

- You’re making 1–5 special cookies for a gift.
- You have steady hands and patience for weeding.
- You’re using a thin mylar stencil (0.05mm).
- You want an elegant, hand-lettered look.

**Recommended script fonts:** Bickham Script, Dancing Script (available on [Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com)), or a custom SVG from [Font Squirrel](https://www.fontsquirrel.com).

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Even with the right font, mistakes happen. Here’s how to recover.

### Tearing During Weeding

If your stencil tears, you’re pulling too hard or the font stroke is too thin. Switch to a cookie font or use a lighter weeding motion.

**Fix:** Apply transfer tape over the stencil before weeding. It holds the fragile parts together.

### Icing Bleeding Under the Stencil

Bleeding means the stencil isn’t flush with the cookie. The font stroke might be too thin, or the stencil material is too stiff.

**Fix:** Use a cookie font with thicker walls. Also, press the stencil down firmly with a fondant smoother before flooding.

### Stencil Lifting When You Remove It

This happens when the stencil adhesive isn’t strong enough. Script fonts with thin sections lift easier.

**Fix:** Use a temporary adhesive spray on the back of the stencil. Or choose a cookie font that has more surface area sticking to the cookie.

## Final Verdict: Which Font Wins for Cricut Stencils?

For 90% of Cricut cookie stencil projects, a cookie font is the better choice. It cuts faster, weeds cleaner, and holds up under icing.

Script fonts have their place — but only for small, high-detail batches where you’re willing to spend extra time on weeding and careful application.

**Bottom line:** Start with a cookie font. Master the technique. Then experiment with scripts when you want a fancier look.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use any script font for Cricut stencils?

Not all script fonts cut well as stencils. Look for scripts with heavier stroke weights (at least 1.5mm) and avoid fonts with long, thin swashes. Test a small cut first.

### What cut settings work best for cookie font stencils?

Use a fine-point blade, standard pressure, and 2 multi-cuts for acetate or mylar. For vinyl stencils, set pressure to “less” to avoid cutting through the backing.

### How do I prevent tiny islands from falling out of script font stencils?

Apply clear transfer tape over the entire stencil before weeding. The tape holds islands in place. Remove the tape after weeding but before placing the stencil on the cookie.

### Which stencil material is best for cookie fonts?

Mylar (0.05–0.1mm) is ideal because it’s flexible and reusable. Acetate works but can crack after a few uses. Vinyl is fine for one-time use but may leave residue.

### Can I adjust a script font to make it stencil-friendly in Cricut Design Space?

Yes. Use the “Offset” tool to thicken the font by 1–2mm. Then weld the offset layers together. This creates a bolder version that cuts and weeds better.
