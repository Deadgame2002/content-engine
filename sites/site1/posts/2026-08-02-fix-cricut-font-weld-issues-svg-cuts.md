If your welded Cricut fonts are coming out with weird gaps, overlapping lines, or broken cuts, you're not alone. Weld issues happen to every crafter at some point. The good news? You can fix them in minutes with the right steps. This guide walks you through the 7 most effective fixes for Cricut font weld problems, so your SVG cuts look flawless on every project.

## Key Takeaways

- Welding combines overlapping shapes into one clean cut line — but only if you do it right.
- The #1 cause of weld issues is using fonts that are too thin or too detailed for the size you're cutting.
- Always check your design in **Contour** and **Slice** modes before cutting to catch hidden problems.
- A quick fix for stubborn gaps is to manually add a small shape to bridge the break.
- If you're using free fonts, verify they have proper outlines — some don't weld correctly at all.

## What Causes Cricut Font Weld Issues?

Weld issues happen when Design Space can't merge overlapping shapes cleanly. The result? Gaps, jagged edges, or letters that fall apart when cut.

Common culprits:

- **Thin or hairline strokes** — they disappear when welded.
- **Fonts with open paths** — the weld tool needs closed shapes to work.
- **Too much overlap** — letters merge into a blob instead of clean lines.
- **Wrong sizing** — welding at 1 inch vs. 10 inches changes how the weld behaves.

Think of weld like glue. It joins shapes together, but if the shapes are too delicate, the glue breaks. Knowing this helps you pick the right fix fast.

## Step 1: Check Your Font Selection

Not all fonts are weld-friendly. Script fonts with thick, consistent strokes weld beautifully. Ultra-thin fonts or those with lots of decorative flourishes often fail.

**What to look for:**

- Fonts with **bold or medium weight** — they have enough meat to weld.
- **Smooth curves** — avoid fonts with sharp, spiky details.
- **Closed loops** — letters like 'o' and 'e' need to be complete shapes.

If you're using a free font from [Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com) or [Font Squirrel](https://www.fontsquirrel.com), check if it has a **.ttf** or **.otf** format. Both work in Cricut, but some free fonts have broken paths. Try a different font — it's the quickest fix.

**Pro tip:** Use a **Cricut font** specifically designed for cutting, like those found on Creative Fabrica. They're tested for weld compatibility.

## Step 2: Adjust Letter Spacing Before Welding

Tight letter spacing is key for a good weld. If your letters are too far apart, they won't overlap enough to weld into one piece.

Here's how to fix it:

1. Select your text in Design Space.
2. Click **Letter Spacing** in the top toolbar.
3. Drag the slider to the left (negative values) until letters overlap slightly.
4. Aim for a **0.5–1 mm overlap** — enough to connect without making a mess.

For script fonts, you want the tail of one letter to touch the next letter's start. That's the sweet spot.

## Step 3: Combine Overlapping Shapes Manually

Sometimes the weld tool just won't cooperate. When that happens, you can manually create the same effect.

**Method:**

1. Duplicate your text (Ctrl+D or Cmd+D).
2. Select both copies and click **Weld** — this sometimes forces the merge.
3. If that fails, use the **Slice** tool to cut out the overlap area, then weld the pieces together.

Another trick: **add a thin rectangle** behind the letters like a baseline. Weld everything together — the rectangle bridges any gaps and disappears into the design.

## Step 4: Fix Gaps Between Letters

Gaps are the most common weld issue. They happen when letters don't touch enough or when the font has internal white spaces.

**Quick fixes:**

- Increase **negative letter spacing** (Step 2).
- Use the **Offset** tool to add a thin outline around the text, then weld that outline.
- **Ungroup** the letters and nudge them closer manually.

For stubborn gaps, draw a small circle or rectangle, place it over the gap, and weld. This fills the hole invisibly.

## Step 5: Use Contour to Remove Unwanted Pieces

Sometimes welding creates stray shapes — like little dots or extra lines from flourishes. The **Contour** tool lets you hide them.

**How to use Contour:**

![Close-up of a weeding tool lifting vinyl from a cutting mat](https://i.ibb.co/YFJrRPCV/site1-fix-cricut-font-weld-issues-svg-cuts-body.jpg)

1. Select your welded design.
2. Click **Contour** in the bottom toolbar.
3. You'll see all the paths. Click on any stray shape to hide it.
4. Only the visible paths will cut.

This is perfect for cleaning up script fonts with extra swooshes that don't weld well.

## Step 6: Slice to Create Custom Welded Shapes

If you need a specific shape — like a circle with text inside — **Slice** is your friend. It cuts one shape out of another.

**Example:** Making a round logo with welded text.

1. Create a circle.
2. Type your text and weld it into one piece.
3. Place the welded text over the circle.
4. Select both and click **Slice**.
5. Delete the unwanted pieces — you're left with a perfect badge.

Slice is more precise than weld for this, and it avoids the messy overlaps that weld can create.

## Step 7: Test Cut on Scrap Vinyl

Never skip the test cut! It saves you from wasting good material.

1. Load a piece of scrap vinyl into your Cricut.
2. Use the **Test Cut** feature (it's in the machine settings).
3. Cut your welded design at the exact size you plan to use.
4. Weed it and inspect for gaps or breaks.

If it looks good, you're golden. If not, go back to Step 2 and adjust spacing.

## Quick Comparison: Weld vs. Slice for Fonts

| Tool | Best For | Pros | Cons |
|------|----------|------|------|
| **Weld** | Combining overlapping text into one path | Simple, fast, preserves shape | Can create blobs with too much overlap |
| **Slice** | Cutting one shape from another | Precise, great for badges | Only works with two layers at a time |
| **Contour** | Removing unwanted parts | Non-destructive, easy undo | Doesn't merge shapes |

Use **Weld** for script words, **Slice** for logos, and **Contour** for cleanup. That's the winning combo.

## How to Avoid Weld Issues in Future Projects

Prevention is better than fixing. Here are my go-to habits:

- **Always use thick, bold fonts** for small cuts (under 2 inches).
- **Stick to fonts from reputable sources** like Creative Fabrica or Google Fonts — they have proper outlines.
- **Check your design at 100% zoom** before cutting.
- **Never weld text that's smaller than 1 inch** — it'll be too fragile.
- **Save a copy of your design** before welding, so you can revert if needed.

## FAQ

### Why does my welded Cricut font have gaps between letters?

Gaps happen when letters don't overlap enough. Reduce letter spacing (make it more negative) until letters touch slightly. If that doesn't work, manually nudge letters closer or add a small shape to bridge the gap.

### Can I fix a weld issue after cutting?

No, once it's cut, you can't change it. That's why test cuts are essential. If you already cut a bad piece, recut with a more overlapping or thicker font.

### What's the best font type for welding?

Script fonts with thick, consistent strokes work best. Avoid thin, serif, or decorative fonts with lots of fine details. Look for fonts labeled "Bold" or "Semi-Bold" — they weld like a dream.

### Does weld work on SVGs?

Yes, you can weld SVG shapes, but they need to overlap. If they don't, weld won't do anything. Use **Ungroup** first to separate paths, then select the overlapping ones and weld.

### Why does my text look like a blob after welding?

That's too much overlap. Undo the weld, increase letter spacing slightly, or reduce the size of the text. Aim for a delicate but visible overlap — about 0.5mm is perfect.
